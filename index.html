From Internal Happiness to External Buzz: A Conceptual Framework Linking Corporate Happiness Responsibility to Consumer Responses

Status: Working Draft (Comparative Analysis)

Note: Green text indicates additions to the original submission.

Abstract

This conceptual paper develops a measurement-oriented framework explaining how Corporate Happiness Responsibility (CHR), defined as organizational investments in employee well-being, translates into consumer responses. We challenge the implicit assumption that internal well-being initiatives automatically enhance external brand perceptions (cf. the "Doing Good leads to Doing Well" fallacy). Instead, we propose that this relationship is conditional on perceived authenticity. We introduce an Authenticity Mediation Model in which CHR operates as a corporate signal, perceived authenticity functions as an interpretive filter, and consumer responses emerge as both evaluative outcomes, such as trust, and behavioral outcomes, such as word of mouth (WOM). The framework identifies three pathways—quality signaling, authenticity evaluation, and trust erosion—each associated with distinct psychological mechanisms. To enable empirical validation, we propose three constructs: the CHR-WOM Spillover Index, the Authenticity Filter Score, and the Internal-External Gap Metric.

1. Core Argument

The central claim is that Corporate Happiness Responsibility does not directly translate into positive consumer outcomes. Instead, it functions as a signal that is interpreted by consumers under conditions of partial observability and uncertainty. While traditional CSR often focuses on highly visible environmental or philanthropic actions, CHR is inherently "veiled" within the organizational black box. As a result, identical CHR investments may generate divergent outcomes depending on how they are evaluated. This shifting role of the corporation suggests that happiness is not just an internal HR metric, but a fundamental evolution of corporate responsibility (Tsaban & Shavit, 2026).

2. Conceptualization of CHR

Corporate Happiness Responsibility is defined as organizational investments and policies aimed at improving employee well-being, including mental health support, work-life balance, compensation practices, and employee development. Research indicate that such investments in well-being are not merely altruistic but are potentially beneficial for overall brand equity (Tsaban & Shavit, 2025).

CHR is conceptualized as a signal with three defining features:

  • it originates internally
  • it is not directly observable to consumers visible at the point of sale.
  • it requires active inference from secondary cues (e.g., Glassdoor ratings or media leaks).

3. Authenticity as the Key Mechanism

Perceived authenticity is defined as the extent to which consumers perceive alignment between internal corporate practices and their external representations. Consumers often experience ambivalence when assessing a firm's responsibility engagement, particularly when they suspect a gap between corporate claims and social realities (Toder-Alon et al., 2019).

Authenticity functions as an interpretive filter that determines whether CHR signals are:

  • accepted as credible
  • discounted as symbolic (or "well-washing")
  • or rejected as inconsistent

4. Outcome Structure

We distinguish between two outcome layers:

  1. Brand trust: A psychological evaluation reflecting perceived reliability and integrity.
  2. Word of mouth: The external expression of consumer evaluations. We conceptualize WOM not just as a volume metric, but as a complex set of rhetorics used in social media talk (Toder-Alon et al., 2016).
    In online communities, these rhetorics evolve as the community matures, shifting the interpersonal influence of CHR signals (Toder-Alon & Brunel, 2007; Alon et al., 2002).

6. Three Pathways

6.1 Quality Signal Pathway

Mechanism: heuristic inference. Consumers infer that firms that treat employees well produce better products or services.

Proposition 1: Higher levels of CHR increase perceived quality and trust through heuristic processing especially in service-intensive industries.

6.2 Authenticity Evaluation Pathway

Mechanism: congruence evaluation and skepticism. The strength of this evaluation is moderated by the consumer's identity salience and their relationship with the brand community (Shuv-Ami & Toder-Alon, 2023).

6.3 Trust Erosion Pathway

Mechanism: discrepancy detection and institutional mistrust. When consumers detect discrepancies, they may move from mere skepticism to active brand hatred or aggression, particularly in highly engaged communities (Shuv-Ami & Toder-Alon, 2022; Icekson et al., 2021).
